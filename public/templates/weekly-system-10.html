SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Intro

Welcome

Weekly System #10

This week is dedicated to imagination. The ability for a child to picture in their head something that doesn't exist — to turn a pillow into a boat, a stick into a dragon, themselves into a prince or a wolf. Ages 2–4 are exactly when imagination explodes: the two-year-old says for the first time “I'm a bear!” and means it; the three-year-old has an imaginary friend; the four-year-old can tell a story with a beginning, middle, and end.

This week feeds that explosion — it doesn't steer it. Seven different ways into the world of imagination:

  • pretending (one thing = another)
  • story structure (beginning, end)
  • imaginary journeys
  • dramatic play (characters, voices)
  • visual storytelling
  • imaginary friends
  • authorship (“my book”)

A note for parents: imagination isn't an escape from reality — imagination is practice for reality. A child who can turn a spoon into a rocket will solve a real problem more easily later, because their brain is used to seeing alternatives. This week may look the least “useful” of all, and that's exactly why it may be the most important. Let the house become a boat. Let the bear talk. Trust your child's imagination and it will give the world back to you.

Guide

How to use
this system

01

Follow, don't steer

When your child says “this is a rocket,” it is a rocket. Don't correct them (“but it's a spoon”). Imagination dies the moment it gets fixed.

02

Yes, and…

The improv rule: whatever your child offers, you build on it, not next to it. “Yes, and the bear is hungry!”

03

Ask “what next?”

An open question that doesn't suggest the answer. Your child leads the story.

04

Save the artifact

Drawings, the storybook, a recording of the story — keep it. A month later, it becomes a memory.

It doesn't matter that the story makes sense.
What matters is that your child feels — my imagined thing exists, someone hears it, someone believes it.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 1 / 7
1

What is this, really?

The spoon isn't a spoon — what is it?

This isn't a spoon. So what is it?

⚡ WOW Mission

Transform an object

Materials: any everyday objects — a spoon, a towel, a box, paper, a sock.

How to play

Pick up an ordinary spoon: “This isn't a spoon. This is… what do you think?” Your child guesses: a microphone? a boat? an airplane? Accept whatever your child says — and use the object that way. If it's a microphone, you “sing” into the spoon. If it's a boat, it “sails” across the table. Then go around the room: towel, box, sock, paper. The ability for an ordinary object to “become something else” is the foundation of all later imagination — your child learns the world isn't fixed, that things can be more than one. In five minutes the whole house becomes transformable. Throughout the day your child points at things and says “this is now…!”

🔧 Medium

One object, three roles

From the same spoon, invent three different things in a row: microphone → boat → giant's ladle. For each one, a short mini-scene. A flexibility workout: same thing, three worlds.

🌿 Easy

You transform

You make the suggestions, your child follows. “This is an airplane! Whoooosh!” “This is food for the bear!” Younger kids only need to feel the act of pretending through your lead — they'll start on their own soon.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 2 / 7
2

Once upon a time…

Character. Place. Event. Story.

Once there was a fish… who lived in the kitchen…

⚡ WOW Mission

Three cards — one story

Materials: 9 small slips of paper, a marker.

How to play

On 3 slips draw characters (bear, child, fish), on 3 draw places (forest, ocean, kitchen), and on 3 draw events (lost, storm, birthday). Your child picks one from each pile. “Once there was a fish… who lived in the kitchen… and one day she was lost!” Ask questions: “What did she do then? Who helped? How did she find her way?” A story with structure is a developmental leap — your child realizes every story has a beginning (who, where), middle (problem), and end (solution). With cards, your child doesn't have to invent from nothing — they build from three given things, ideal for a small working memory. Record or write the story down — your child will listen again.

🔧 Medium

Change the ending

Same three slips, same story — but near the end ask: “What if the fish had decided differently?” Your child builds an alternate ending. Practice in imagination and in the idea that stories have choices.

🌿 Easy

Once upon a bear

No cards. You start: “Once upon a time, a bear.” Your child adds one word. You add the next. Take turns. Younger kids only need to feel the rhythm of a story through that back-and-forth.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 3 / 7
3

Journey under the blanket

I see a fish! It's huge!

Let's take a trip. What is this — a car, a boat, a rocket?

⚡ WOW Mission

A vehicle from a blanket

Materials: a big blanket, two chairs (or a table + a sheet).

How to play

Drape a big blanket across two chairs = “a vehicle.” “Let's take a trip! What is this — a car, a boat, a rocket, an elephant?” Your child picks. “Where are we going?” They pick: to the moon, deep underwater, into the forest, to grandma's. The whole “trip” happens under the blanket. You make sounds (engine, waves, rockets), describe what you see (“I see a fish! It's huge!”), and ask your child what they see. You travel for 5–10 minutes and “arrive.” Imagination thrives inside a held frame. The blanket gives a physical space inside which everything can be true. Your child feels safe (hidden) and free (everywhere) at the same time. The blanket and two chairs become a portal.

🔧 Medium

Trip map

Before leaving, your child dictates the route: “First the moon, then the ocean, then grandma's.” You sketch the map on paper. You travel through every stop in order. Practice with sequence and planning inside imagination.

🌿 Easy

Hiding

No trip. Just the blanket — you and your child inside. Talk about what you sense (“it's dark,” “it's warm”). For younger kids, the physical space under the blanket is enough for a first step toward imagination.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 4 / 7
4

Bear voice, mouse voice

HELLO! / heeellooo!

The big bear talks in a deep voice. The little mouse…

⚡ WOW Mission

A conversation between characters

Materials: two stuffed animals of different sizes (one big, one small).

How to play

“This big bear talks like this (deep voice): HELLO!” “And the little mouse talks like this (high voice): heeellooo!” Your child copies you. Then a conversation: bear says something, mouse answers. You can hold the bear, your child the mouse — or swap. Add a third character (bunny — quick voice, lion — slow voice). Changing voices is an early workout in dramatic imagination — the ability to be someone else. A child who can switch voices will later be able to understand different characters in books, movies, and even in life — that mom talks differently at grandma's than at the bank. Your child howls with laughter at their own deep bear voice.

🔧 Medium

Three characters and a scene

Three stuffed animals, three voices, a small scene: bear is hungry, mouse has cheese, bunny brings water. Your child speaks for one or two of them, you speak for the rest. Practice holding several characters at once.

🌿 Easy

Imitate me

You make one voice (deep: “mmmmoooo”), your child imitates. Then another (thin: “eeeeee”). No character, no scene — just play with sound. A simple doorway into drama for younger kids.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 5 / 7
5

A drawing comes alive

Who lives in the house? What's the bunny doing?

A word becomes a picture. A picture becomes a story.

⚡ WOW Mission

A drawing-story

Materials: a larger sheet of paper (or the back of a paper bag), a marker.

How to play

Draw something simple — a house with a door and a window, a tree, a sun. “This is a house. Who lives in the house?” Your child says (a bunny, grandma, a princess, a dinosaur). You draw it. “What's the bunny doing?” Your child: “Sleeping.” You draw the bunny in bed. “What does it hear?” “Knocking on the door.” You draw whoever's knocking. The story grows with the drawing. Your child sees their words turn into pictures — imagination becomes visible. Plus, this is early exposure to the idea that a story can be recorded, kept, brought back. By the end, the drawing is a complete world your child invented. Hang it on the wall.

🔧 Medium

You draw

Your child dictates the whole scene from the start: “Draw the ocean. Then a fish. Then a captain.” No questions from you — your child leads. Practice in full authorship and a clear vision.

🌿 Easy

Sun and house

A simple scene — sun, house, one creature. Your child names what to draw (“a cat!”), you draw. For younger kids, linking word and drawing is wonder enough — no story needed.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 6 / 7
6

Invisible friend at tea

Pip, would you like some milk?

I have an idea. Let's invite an invisible friend over.

⚡ WOW Mission

Invite an invisible guest

Materials: a couple of cups, small spoons, “food” (could be a cookie or play dough).

How to play

“I have an idea. Let's invite an invisible friend over for tea.” Your child picks a name (“Pip,” “Lulu,” “Sparky”). You set out a cup for the invisible guest. Pretend to talk with them seriously: “Pip, would you like some milk? What? You say you love cookies?” Your child joins in. Then the invisible friend “does” something silly (spills the tea, eats all the cookies, hides under the table). Imaginary friends are a normal and healthy part of development at ages 2–4. Studies show kids who play with imaginary friends develop better emotional regulation and a richer vocabulary. A parent who supports the imaginary world, instead of laughing it off, builds a child who trusts their own thoughts.

🔧 Medium

What does Pip do tomorrow?

A recurring imaginary friend. Your child tells what Pip does during the day, what Pip likes, what Pip is afraid of. You speak with Pip as if Pip is real. Your child runs the entire cosmology — practice in long imagination and story continuity.

🌿 Easy

Bear talks

No invisible guest — use a stuffed animal as the “invisible” one. The bear “says” something through your voice, your child answers. For younger kids, a physically present stuffed animal is a safer step before a fully imaginary friend.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #10 · getscreenfree.com Day 7 / 7
7

Our big storybook

A storybook by (your child).

My stories. My book. My name.

⚡ WOW Mission

Make a book

Materials: a few sheets of paper folded or stapled together, a marker, a little time.

How to play

Make a book together. Fold a few sheets of paper in half, sew or staple them. On the cover: “A storybook by (child's name).” Inside — your child dictates stories they made up all week (or new ones). You write them down, leaving space for drawings your child adds. At the end you sit together and “read” the book aloud. Your child sees their own authored work for the first time. The words they invented exist on paper. The book stays. A month later, your child pulls it out and asks for it to be “read.” The moment an author is born. Parents often keep this book for years — it becomes a keepsake.

🔧 Medium

With titles

Every story gets a title. Your child invents it: “Fish in the kitchen.” “Bear and mouse.” Practice in summarizing — the ability to capture a whole story in a few words.

🌿 Easy

Three pages

For younger kids, two or three pages is plenty. One sentence per page. A drawing your child adds — a small mark or scribble. The book doesn't have to be big to be theirs.
