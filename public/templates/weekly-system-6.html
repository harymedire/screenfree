Welcome

Weekly System #6

This week is devoted to speech and conversation. Seven days, seven different ways to build vocabulary, rhythm, and narrative — through games that look like cuddling, laughter, or ordinary daily life:

  • naming the world and the “+1” technique
  • dialogic reading (CROWD prompts)
  • rhythm, song, and pause
  • conversation through a real “telephone”
  • animal sounds and laughing at mistakes
  • adjectives through touch
  • telling the day in three little pictures

A note for the parent: this week is different from the others. Less “what to do,” more “how to be.” Success isn't measured by whether your child gives the “right answer” — it's measured by how much conversation happened. One word from your child after your question is a win. Five words is a luxury. Silence isn't failure — silence listens too.

Each day leans on concrete research from developmental psychology — from the Hart & Risley study on early vocabulary, through Whitehurst's dialogic reading, to Reese & Fivush on autobiographical memory. You don't need to know these names — the week works without them.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com How to use

Guide

How to use
this system

01

Less hurry

This week isn't a do-it-and-move-on. An activity can last 5 or 25 minutes — as long as your child stays in the conversation.

02

The pause is magic

After a question — wait. Kids ages 2–4 need 5–10 seconds to form an answer. Silence is part of the conversation.

03

The +1 technique

Whatever your child says, repeat it and add one word. “Car” → “Red car.” “Dog” → “Dog runs.”

04

No corrections

If your child says “me want water” instead of “I want water” — don't correct them. Just repeat it correctly: “Yes, here's some water.” That's enough.

The most important thing isn't that everything be perfect.
What matters is that your child feels — my words matter, someone is listening to what I say.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 1 / 7
1

Name the World

Slow looking — and one extra word.

Your child points. You name. And add one.

⚡ WOW Mission

Slow Looking (+1)

Materials: just your child in your lap and the room around you.

How to play

Take your child in your lap and slowly, in a whisper, point to objects in the room. “See the lamp? The lamp is shining. It's warm.” Pause. “And this? A book. Your book.” No rush, no test. Your child points, you name — and add one extra word beyond what they say. If they say “car,” you say “red car” or “car drives.” It doesn't look like an activity, it looks like cuddling — yet it's the most powerful teaching of the week. The “+1” technique: kids whose parents consistently add one word more develop a significantly larger vocabulary by age three.

🔧 Medium

What I See, What I Hear

Walk through the house. Name what you see and hear: “I see a red apple. I hear water in the bathroom.” Your child follows and tries to repeat or add their own. “What do you see? What do you hear?” Practices attention, speech, and linking the visual with the auditory.

🌿 Easy

One Word

No walking. Sit together, look out the window or around the room. Name one object and wait: “Bird.” Your child repeats or points to something else. You name that one. Slow, calm, no pressure — perfect before bedtime.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 2 / 7
2

Wordless Book

The picture book becomes a conversation.

The picture asks the question. You listen for the answer.

⚡ WOW Mission

Dialogic Reading

Materials: any picture book from home.

How to play

Instead of reading the text, ask CROWD prompts — five evidence-based types of dialogic reading: Completion (let them finish: “The bear is...”), Recall (“What was the bear doing on the last page?”), Open (“What's happening here?”), Wh-questions (“Where? Who? What?”), Distancing (“Have you ever...?”). With younger kids just C and Wh; older ones, all five. Studies show measurable vocabulary gains after just 4 weeks of this kind of reading.

🔧 Medium

Page = One Scene

Go page by page. One question on each: “What do you see? Who's there? What do you think is happening?” Don't rush to finish the book — two pages in twenty minutes of conversation is plenty.

🌿 Easy

Point and Name

No questions. You point and name: “This is the bear. The bear is sleeping.” Your child listens and points. For younger kids, pointing with a finger is enough — that's their speech.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 3 / 7
3

Humming and Rhythm

The body follows the sound.

Sound enters the hands. Hands tap the rhythm.

⚡ WOW Mission

Song With a Pause

Materials: a familiar song you both know (Twinkle Twinkle, Itsy Bitsy Spider, Wheels on the Bus).

How to play

Sit face to face. Clap hands, tap knees, “tap-tap” on the table — hum a familiar song while you tap the rhythm. Vary the tempo: fast, slow, quiet, loud. Then stop in the middle of the song — your child finishes it: “Twinkle twinkle little...?” → “Star!” They'll laugh until they cry when you “forget” the word. You'll discover your child remembers songs you didn't think they knew. Rhythmic awareness is a precursor to phonological awareness — the strongest early predictor of later reading success.

🔧 Medium

Tap for an Answer

You tap a rhythm (3 fast, pause, 1 long). Your child copies it. Then switch — your child leads, you follow. Practices listening, sequence memory, and motor control.

🌿 Easy

Tap-Tap Together

No song. Just tap together on the table or knees in rhythm. Your child copies your tempo. If they can't — do it together, hand over hand. A small but important rhythm the body remembers.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 4 / 7
4

String Telephone

The voice travels through the string.

Hello? Can you hear me?

⚡ WOW Mission

A Real Telephone

Materials: two plastic cups, thin sturdy string (about 6 ft), a knot at each end.

How to play

Poke a small hole in the bottom of each cup, thread the string through both, and tie a knot on the inside of each. Stretch the string tight — now it's a “telephone.” Talk from opposite ends of the room. “Hello, Mom!” “Yes, I can hear you!” “Goodbye!” An old trick kids haven't seen — the vibration really does carry sound. The medium itself is the reward — your child chooses words more carefully because they're “sending” a message. A small jump in motivation to talk that you don't get from ordinary conversation.

🔧 Medium

Send a Message

Don't talk freely — give your child a “mission”: “Tell me what you ate for breakfast.” Your child calls and tells. “Tell me your favorite toy.” Practices short, focused speech and choosing words.

🌿 Easy

Hello and Goodbye

Younger kids use the telephone as a toy — they don't need a real conversation. “Hello!” then “Goodbye!” then “Hello!” again. Repeating social phrases is gold for a 2-year-old.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 5 / 7
5

Animal Sounds

The cow says... meow? Laughter, then a correction.

Mom got it wrong. The kid knows better.

⚡ WOW Mission

Deliberate Mistakes

Materials: any animals (toys, books, or just from memory).

How to play

“What does the cow say? The dog? The duck? The cat?” Your child makes the sounds. Then build on it: how they move, where they live, what they eat. Then get it wrong on purpose: “The cow says meow!” Your child laughs and corrects you. “The dog flies!” — “No, the dog runs!” A parent's mistakes activate metalinguistic awareness — your child notices for the first time that there is a “right” and “wrong” in language. Plus, correcting an adult gives them a sense of competence and boosts their motivation to talk.

🔧 Medium

Movement + Sound

Imitate animals — move like a cow (slow, heavy), like a duck (waddle), like a frog (hop). Add the sound. “Now we're a bunny!” “Now we're a bear!” Combines language, movement, and imitation in one game.

🌿 Easy

Who Says What?

No movement, no mistakes. You show an animal (toy or picture) and ask: “Who is this? What do they say?” Your child imitates the sound. Younger kids adore this simple game — and repeat it on their own long after.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 6 / 7
6

Treasure Bag

Ohhh, soft! Try it — soft.

What's in the bag? The hand finds out first.

⚡ WOW Mission

Words Through Touch

Materials: a bag or box; 5–6 everyday objects with different qualities: a hard block, a soft stuffed animal, a cold spoon, a rough sponge, a light feather, a smooth cup.

How to play

Your child pulls one object out at a time. Don't ask “what's it like” (too abstract for a 2-year-old) — instead, model it: “Ohhh, soft! Try it — soft.” Your child feels it and repeats the word. Gradually, through repetition over weeks, your child starts describing on their own. Adjectives are the hardest category of words for little kids — but exposure through touch + word + repetition is the most effective way to absorb them (multisensory learning).

🔧 Medium

Find Something Soft

Hunt for an object by description: “Find something soft. Find something cold. Something heavy.” Your child picks. Practices linking adjectives to objects — and the other way around, associating qualities with things in the house.

🌿 Easy

Just Touch

No adjectives. Your child pulls out an object and explores it with their hands — you just name the object: “Spoon. Sponge. Feather.” That's enough for very young kids — the word for the object is already a lot.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com Day 7 / 7
7

Our Day in Three Pictures

Remember? What did we do?

Three little pictures. The whole day.

⚡ WOW Mission

Drawings of Today

Materials: paper, a pencil or marker.

How to play

Toward the end of the day, sit with your child: “Let's remember what we did today.” Draw three things that happened, very simply (breakfast, a walk, bath time — stick figures are plenty). Show them one at a time: “Remember? What did we do?” Your child adds a word or two. You expand: “Yes, we ate bread and cheese. The cheese was yellow.” This is narrative practice — telling the story of what was experienced — proven to be linked to later literacy and emotional regulation. The moment autobiographical memory is born.

🔧 Medium

Five Pictures + Order

Draw five things, then mix them up on the table. Your child puts them in the order they happened: morning, lunch, afternoon, dinner, bath. “What was first? What came after?” Practices sequence and memory.

🌿 Easy

One Thing Today

No drawing. Ask your child: “What did we do today that you liked best?” Your child says one word — “bath,” “dog,” “cookie.” You expand: “Yes, we saw a dog at the park. He was big.” Simplest version, perfect before bed.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #6 · getscreenfree.com SOS ideas

Ready for in-the-moment

SOS ideas
when your child asks for a screen

When your child asks for a screen out of boredom or habit, try quickly starting a short conversation. One question that calls for attention, memory, or imitation brings your child back into contact with you:

“See this? Can you tell me what it is?”

“What do you think is happening in this picture?”

“Hum with me — twinkle, twinkle…”

“What does the duck say? And the dog?”

“Touch it — what's it like? Soft? Hard?”

“Remember — what did we eat this morning?”

“Tell me something about the bear.”

“The cow says meow? (Laugh.)”

A conversation doesn't have to be long.
One question, one answer, one bit of praise — and your child is somewhere else.
