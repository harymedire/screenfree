Welcome

Weekly System #9

This week is all about togetherness. The first eight weeks have been building your child — movement, discovery, the senses, language, feelings, thinking. Week nine is about what happens when your child meets another being — another child, a sibling, a stuffed animal, or you yourself as “the other player.”

This is the territory of sharing, taking turns, helping, and games with rules — exactly the things parents of 2–4-year-olds wrestle with at the playground, at birthday parties, at grandma's. Not as a manners drill — as play that naturally brings those moments. The week builds:

  • giving instead of just receiving
  • taking turns
  • contributing to the family
  • tuning in with others
  • empathy through practice
  • understanding another's perspective
  • gratitude and positive memories

Note for the parent: this week isn't about “teaching them to share.” It isn't about manners. It isn't about your child being “good.” This is a week of shared moments — between your child and you, stuffed animals, and others in the home. Let the activities be short. Let them be imperfect. Everything worthwhile is built through many small repetitions, not through one big day.

1

A Cup of Tea for the Bear

The little host sets the table.

Thank you, little host. Mmm, sweet tea!

⚡ WOW Mission

Little Host

Materials: cups (empty or with a little water), small spoons, “cookies” (crackers, fruit, or shaped playdough), 2–3 stuffed animals.

How to play

Your child prepares “tea” for the stuffed animals. Cups, spoons, cookies. Your child serves: the bear, the bunny, the doll, mommy. You voice the bear: “Ohhh, thank you! Mmmm, sweet tea!” Then the bunny gets some: “May I have some too? I'm thirsty!” Your child gives to each one in turn. For the first time, your child gives to others, not just receives. Stuffed animals are a safe first audience — they don't grab, cry, or get angry. Through their voices you introduce “thank you,” “please,” “here you go.” Your child learns these words without anyone “teaching” them. Parents melt — a two-year-old acting like a little host.

🔧 Medium

A Real Little Party

Party rules: “Everyone gets some first, then we eat.” “Who wants milk? Who wants tea?” Your child keeps track of guests — who got what, who didn't. Practice in noticing others and remembering.

🌿 Easy

Give the Bear a Drink

No guests, no rules — just the bear and a cup. “Give the bear a drink.” Your child gives, you voice the bear sipping. For younger kids, just feeling the act of giving through one simple motion is enough.

Your Turn, My Turn

The ball goes there. Then back. Then there.

My turn. Now yours. Then mine. Then yours.

⚡ WOW Mission

Ball Back-and-Forth

Materials: one ball (or a block, a small car), open space between you and your child.

How to play

Sit facing each other. “My turn — I'm rolling.” Gently roll the ball to your child. “Now your turn.” Your child rolls it back. Then expand: your child picks a toy, and you take turns pulling — your child pulls a block from the box, then you, then your child. The whole game is in waiting your turn. Rolling is the easiest entry — visible motion, a clear handover moment. Every later social skill (waiting in line, not interrupting, sharing) grows out of this small ritual. In 5 minutes your child slips into a rhythm that the playground hasn't taught in months.

🔧 Medium

Who's Next?

Three or four “players” — you, your child, a stuffed animal, mom (or a doll). Roll the ball in a circle. Question between rounds: “Who's after you?” Your child predicts the order. Practice with sequence memory and waiting through multiple turns.

🌿 Easy

Just Roll

No “mine” or “yours” — just rolling back and forth. For younger kids, the motion toward-and-away is enough on its own. The word “turn” arrives later — first comes the rhythm.

3

Helper of the Day

I couldn't do it without you. Thank you.

I need help. Will you be my helper?

⚡ WOW Mission

Real Tasks

Materials: real household tasks that are safe for your child (napkins, socks, pillows, spoons).

How to play

You say: “I need help today. Will you be my helper?” Your child gets tasks that are real, not made-up: put napkins on the table, bring shoes from the hall, drop socks in the laundry, move a pillow, set spoons on the table. Each task — praise. “I couldn't do it without you. Thank you.” A 2–4-year-old desperately wants to help, but parents often think it's faster to do it themselves. When your child gets a real chance to contribute, confidence grows — and they learn that they're part of a family that works together, not just a recipient of care. A moment worth a photo.

🔧 Medium

Chain of Tasks

Three tasks in sequence: “First bring the shoes. Then put them there. Then tell me when you're done.” Your child remembers and carries them out in order. Practice with sequencing + independence. Don't correct the order even if they slip — just comment at the end.

🌿 Easy

One Task

For younger kids, just one simple task: “Can you bring me that spoon?” Big praise when they do it. Small task, big pride.

We Sing Together

A band of spoons and a pot.

Now everyone soft… now everyone loud!

⚡ WOW Mission

Family Band

Materials: wooden spoons + a pot (drums); a shaker (rice in a bottle); a whistle (blowing into an empty bottle); a song you both know.

How to play

Pick up your “instruments.” Sing your favorite song and play together. Then try different roles: “Now you keep the beat, I'll sing.” “Now I play, you conduct (wave your hands).” “Now everyone soft... now everyone loud!” Making music together is one of the oldest forms of social bonding in our species. Your child learns to sync with another — listening to each other, starting and stopping together. A precursor to every team game. The living room becomes a stage; other family members join in and a real family moment happens.

🔧 Medium

You Conduct

Your child is the conductor. They wave their hands: up = loud, down = soft, hand in a circle = fast, flat = slow. You sing or play exactly how your child shows you. Your child leads — practice with tempo control through another player.

🌿 Easy

One Beat

No song. Tap one simple rhythm together (tap-tap-pause). Your child copies. If they can't — do it together, hand over hand. The simplest synchronizing.

5

A Hug for the Bear

Your hugs heal.

The bear is sad. What does he need?

⚡ WOW Mission

Stuffed-Animal Helper

Materials: 2–3 stuffed animals; a small “bandage” (a scrap of paper), a soft cloth, a blanket.

How to play

The stuffed animals are “sad” this morning. Set the scene: the bear fell and hurt his leg, the bunny got lost, the doll is alone. Your child is the “helper” — comforts, cuddles, gives a hug, finds a bandage, sings a lullaby. Through the stuffed animals' voices you respond: “Thank you, I feel better now. Your hugs heal!” Your child practices caring for someone else within a safe frame. Stuffed animals are the perfect first “other” — they don't push back, they don't reject. Empathy is built through practice, not through explanation. A child who often comforts a bear more easily comforts a younger sibling, a friend at the park, a buddy at daycare.

🔧 Medium

Little Doctor

For an older child, give a more complex scenario: “The bear has three problems — his leg hurts, he's hungry, and he's sad because the bunny left.” Your child solves each problem in turn. Practice in recognizing different needs and priorities.

🌿 Easy

One Hug

One stuffed animal, one comfort. “The bear is crying.” Your child hugs or pats him. For younger kids, just feeling the act of comforting is enough — words and explanations come later.

Pretend You're Mom

Now you're mom. I'm the baby.

Mom, was I a good baby?

⚡ WOW Mission

Role Swap

Materials: nothing special — you can use whatever's at hand.

How to play

You say: “Let's pretend. Now you're mom (or dad), and I'm the baby.” Your child plays the parent: feeds “the baby” (you), dresses you, “puts you to bed,” talks like a parent. Then swap. Then your child plays something else: a doctor, a shopkeeper, a hairdresser. Always follow your child's lead. Role swap may be the most powerful social practice of the preschool years — for the first time your child sees the world through someone else's eyes. They discover that mom isn't only “mom” — that she's a person who speaks, makes decisions, cares. A child playing the parent often reveals what matters to them — what they repeat in play is what they hear at home. The parent hears themselves for the first time, through their child's mouth.

🔧 Medium

A Whole Scene

A longer role with a plot: “A doctor and a patient who doesn't want a shot.” “A hairdresser and a customer who wants a silly haircut.” Your child leads the conversation and the scene. Practice in sustained perspective and flexible imagination.

🌿 Easy

Feed Me

The simplest scene: your child is “mom,” you are “the baby.” “Feed me.” Your child pretends to feed you. Short, safe, funny — an entry into the world of pretend.

7

A Big Thank You

Thank you, mom. Thank you, kitty. Thank you, juice.

What was lovely today?

⚡ WOW Mission

Thank-You Book

Materials: a few sheets of paper folded or stapled into a “book,” markers.

How to play

You make a little “thank-you book.” On each page your child draws (or you draw for them) one thing that was lovely today / this week / this month. Mom, grandma, the kitty, juice, a walk, ice cream. Then read aloud together: “Thank you, mom. Thank you, kitty. Thank you, juice.” Gratitude is a skill we learn. A 2–4-year-old doesn't grasp abstract gratitude (“be grateful”), but they understand the concrete (“thank you, kitty, because you're soft”). The book stays as a physical object that comes back, gets read again, gets shown to guests — positive moments are remembered twice over.

🔧 Medium

Thank You With a Reason

On each page, not just “thank you, mom,” but: “Thank you, mom, because you hug me.” “Thank you, kitty, because you purr.” Practice in pairing gratitude with a concrete reason — which makes the feeling tangible.

🌿 Easy

Three Things

No book. Near the end of the day, sit with your child: “Let's remember three lovely moments from today.” Your child names what they can. You help if they're not sure. The simplest version — perfect before bed.

SOS ideas
when your child asks for a screen

When your child asks for a screen, invite them into one shared moment. A helper, a guest, a conductor, a stuffed-animal doctor. Connection is built through many small “togethers”:

“I need a helper — want to be one?”

“Does the bear want some tea?”

“The ball — my turn, your turn?”

“Who can we say thank you to today?”

“Let's sing together — twinkle, twinkle…”

“The bear is sad — can he have a hug?”

“Let's pretend — you're mom.”

“What was lovely today?”

Connection isn't taught — it's repeated.
Small moments, many times.
