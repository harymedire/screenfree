Welcome to the final week

Weekly System #12

This is the final week. You and your child have moved through body, discovery, roles, senses, nature, language, feelings, thinking, togetherness, imagination, and the kitchen. Something has shifted now — in your child and in you. Play is no longer an “activity” you plan; it has become a natural reflex when needed.

This week isn't a new theme — it's integration. It returns to all the major motifs of the previous weeks and gives them their final, warmest shape. Plus, it builds physical anchors — a ritual that has a name, a treasure box, a letter, a diploma — tools that outlast the product. This week builds:

  • creating a family ritual
  • ownership of the environment (the box)
  • authorship of play (your own activity)
  • narrative identity (the album)
  • understanding of time (the letter)
  • independence in choosing (the free day)
  • gratitude and closure

A note for the parent: this is the last week, but not the end. Everything you've built over the past 11 weeks is now part of you. This week isn't about “new activities” — it's about recognizing what has already changed. Your child has changed. You have changed. The play has stayed. What follows after this week isn't a return to the old — it's the new normal.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 1 / 7
1

Our family ritual

Our little hour. Our moment.

When works best for our little moment together?

⚡ WOW Mission

We name the ritual

Materials: a piece of paper, a marker, tape or a magnet (for somewhere visible).

How to play

Together with your child, pick one moment in the day that will be your steady play ritual — 10 minutes after breakfast, before dinner, or right before bed. “Let's have our little moment every day. When works best for you?” Your child chooses. Then you come up with a name together: “Five Minutes of Wonder,” “Our Gateway,” “The Little Hour.” Draw or write the name on paper and post it somewhere visible. A habit with a name, a place, and a time outlasts a habit that's only an intention — by ten times. Your child takes part in defining the ritual, which builds ownership of it. The name becomes a “hook” in everyday life: you say “it's time for our gateway” and your child knows exactly what that means.

🔧 Medium

Rules of the ritual

Together with your child, agree on 2–3 rules for the ritual: no phone, just the two of us, lasts as long as we want. The rules go under the name. A practice in shared agreement.

🌿 Easy

You suggest, your child nods

For younger children, you propose the name (“Our Hour”) — your child nods or tilts their head yes. No voting, no choosing — just introducing a ritual that already exists.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 2 / 7
2

The treasure box

Everything we need for our minutes.

In this box are all our most beautiful minutes.

⚡ WOW Mission

We make the treasure box

Materials: an empty box (a shoe box works); 8–10 small things you used across the previous weeks — string, a name pebble, a feather, a calm jar, a face card, a treasure pouch.

How to play

Bring out the empty box and say: “This is our treasure box. Everything we need for our best play minutes goes inside.” Walk through the house with your child and pick small things from the previous weeks. Close the box and put it in a visible but reachable spot. A real box turns an abstract habit into a real object. When you need an idea, you don't have to remember — you open the box. A child who sees the box associates it with play. The principle is “environment design” — surroundings that lead to the desired behavior. The box becomes a family keepsake — still being used and added to months later.

🔧 Medium

Your child defends the choice

Your child picks the items. For each one, they tell a quick story: “Why this? What did we do?” A practice in connecting an object with a memory.

🌿 Easy

You pick, your child confirms

For younger children, you pick the items and your child confirms with a nod or a “yes.” Short, no pressure. The goal is that the box exists, not that your child runs the process.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 3 / 7
3

Three old, one new

Remember? Now what?

Three favorites. Then one new one — that you make up.

⚡ WOW Mission

Three memories + an original game

Materials: nothing special — just space and 30–60 minutes.

How to play

Your child picks three favorite activities from any of the previous weeks. You can help: “Remember when we made the car wash? Or the bear on your tummy? Or the trip under the blanket?” Your child picks three. Across the day, you do all three again — quick, 10 minutes each. At the end, your child invents a fourth, new activity that doesn't exist in any of the previous weeks. Anything — your child has the idea, you listen. Your child walks through their archive of memories, which deepens the emotional value of past experiences. Plus, your child creates their own activity for the first time — no longer a consumer of the system, now an author. The moment you see your child no longer needs a “guide” for play.

🔧 Medium

Your child runs the whole thing

Without your suggestions. Your child picks the three old activities on their own (“I want the one when we did…”). Then invents the fourth. Maximum authorship.

🌿 Easy

Parent offers options

You suggest 5–6 things from previous weeks. Your child picks three. For younger children, an option is easier than a free choice. Invent the fourth new activity together.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 4 / 7
4

Album of the days

Remember this? What were we doing?

See us? What were we doing here?

⚡ WOW Mission

A trip through memories

Materials: a phone (or paper + markers if there are no photos); 20–30 minutes together.

How to play

Pick up the phone and look through photos and videos from previous weeks together with your child. Sit close, “travel” through your memories: “Remember this? What were we doing?” Your child tells the story in their own words. If there are no photos — draw the most beautiful moments together in a big paper “book” titled “Our 12 Weeks.” A memory that gets retold becomes more lasting. A child who “retells” the past builds a narrative identity — “this is us, this is what we did, this is what we did together.” A moment when you witness how much your child has grown, how much they've learned. Parents often say this is the most beautiful moment of the whole system.

🔧 Medium

Your child tells

Your child tells what they see in their own words. You listen and ask questions: “How did you feel here?” “Who else was there?” A practice in narrating memory.

🌿 Easy

You tell, your child points

For younger children, you tell the story: “See, here we were making dough. You were in the kitchen.” Your child points to what they recognize. No expectation of narration — just looking together.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 5 / 7
5

A letter to your future child

Open when I'm 6 years old.

A letter for someone else. Who is you — just later.

⚡ WOW Mission

A letter in an envelope

Materials: a piece of paper, a marker, an envelope, a safe place to keep it (a box, a drawer).

How to play

Pick up paper: “Let's write a letter. A letter for you — for when you're bigger.” You write (your child dictates or just says the words): “Right now you're (age). You love… Your favorite game is… You laugh when…” Your child adds anything they want to tell their “older self.” Seal the letter in an envelope, write “Open when I'm 6 years old” (or 8, or 10). Tuck it away safely. Your child thinks for the first time of themselves as a being who will change. Understanding time (now, later, when I'm big) is a developmental leap that arrives right around ages 3–4. The letter becomes a gift from a future person — when it's opened, it's an enormous emotional moment for the whole family.

🔧 Medium

Your child dictates

Ask your child: “What do you love right now? What do you want to tell yourself when you're big?” Your child dictates, you write. A practice in self-awareness and talking about time.

🌿 Easy

Parent writes what they see

For younger children, you mostly write what you see in your child: “You love your bear. You laugh when daddy makes silly faces. You love apple juice.” Your child nods or says “yes.” The letter stays; your child doesn't have to understand all of it.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 6 / 7
6

The big day of freedom

What do you want us to do?

Today you choose. Anything you want.

⚡ WOW Mission

Your child leads the day

Materials: nothing — a day with no plan.

How to play

“Today you choose. Anything you want us to do.” Your child leads the entire day (within reason). You follow along, suggesting only when your child asks. The goal: you witness how well your child can now choose and lead play on their own. No screens, no quiet help, no prep. This is the test that isn't a test. If your child can lead the day without screens, with varied play, the system worked. That's when you see the real transformation — not something they “learned,” but something they became. A child who picks their own play is a child who won't reach for a screen as the first option. A day that begins with “what now?” ends with “one more, mom, just one more.”

🔧 Medium

The whole day

For a 4-year-old — the whole day, real freedom. Suggest only when your child asks directly. Watch how many different games your child picks throughout the day. A moment of pride.

🌿 Easy

An hour or two

For younger children, a shorter window — an hour or two. More help. If your child “gets stuck,” remind them about the treasure box or the three old activities from Day 3.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #12 · getscreenfree.comDay 7 / 7
7

Our circle of gratitude

Diploma + a hug.

I'm grateful for your laughter.

⚡ WOW Mission

The circle of gratitude and the diploma

Materials: a diploma you prepare earlier in the week — paper saying “(Child's name) finished 12 weeks of the ScreenFree adventure,” the date, your signature, a little drawing; the whole family (if possible).

How to play

Sit together (the whole family if you can). Make a circle of gratitude — everyone says three things they're grateful for from these 12 weeks. You start, you model: “I'm grateful I had time just for you.” “I'm grateful for our laughter.” “I'm grateful to see how much you've grown.” Your child adds in their own words. The ending — a big hug. Your child receives the diploma. Closing with gratitude lands in the body differently than “we did it.” The diploma is a physical symbol — your child shows it off for days, weeks afterward. A moment that's remembered — for the gratitude, the pride, the feeling that these 12 weeks really did change something.

🔧 Medium

Your child says three things

A 4-year-old can say three things they're grateful for on their own. Don't correct them (“the dog,” “juice,” “mom” are completely fine). It's all valid.

🌿 Easy

One thing, with help

For younger children, one thing, with your help: “Maybe when we…?” Your child nods or says “yes.” The diploma is handed over either way — you finished the weeks, you earned it.

Why 12 weeks?

Why exactly
12 weeks?

Behind this number is real research on how family habits that include both parent and child are formed.

66 days — the average for a new behavior to become automatic (Lally, UCL, 2009)
<1h of daily screen time recommended by the AAP for ages 2–5, with active parent involvement
8–12 weeks of consistent ritual — the minimum for measurable family change

66 days — and why 12 weeks then

A UCL study (Lally et al., 2009) showed that the average time for a new behavior to become automatic is 66 days. 12 weeks = 84 days — 66 plus room for real life: illness, travel, off days. An 84-day system gives a family the slack it needs — which is exactly why it survives.

What pediatricians and the WHO say

The AAP recommends less than one hour of screen time per day for ages 2–5, with active parent involvement. The WHO (2019) recommends no screens before age 2 and a minimum between ages 2 and 4. At this age, children learn through their body, senses, and relationships — a screen offers only sight and demands no active participation. Our system adds to that science what research often doesn't deliver: concrete, doable ideas.

A realistic rhythm and what actually changes

Consistency matters more than completeness — better 3 activities a week for 12 weeks straight than all 7 the first week and nothing after. Studies on family rituals (Fiese, Fivush) show that families who introduce a consistent shared-play ritual for 8–12 weeks report: less reaching for screens, larger child vocabulary, better emotional regulation, and — less parental stress.

What you finished isn't “12 weeks of activities.” It's an 84-day window built on the natural timing of how a family habit forms. If you followed it — even halfway — what happened wasn't an accident.
