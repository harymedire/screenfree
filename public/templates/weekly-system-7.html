SCREENFREE · Weekly System #7 · getscreenfree.com Intro

Welcome

Weekly System #7

This week is dedicated to emotional literacy and self-regulation. The 2–4 age window is exactly when the amygdala is firing at full strength, the prefrontal cortex is just coming online, and your child has huge feelings they can't yet understand. Without tools, that looks like tantrums, biting, withdrawing. With tools — it becomes growth.

Seven days that distill what's scattered across hundreds of academic studies — from “Name it to tame it” (Dan Siegel), through emotion coaching (Gottman) and polyvagal theory (Porges), to the gratitude practice from positive psychology. You don't need to know any of these names — the week works without them.

  • noticing feelings on a face
  • naming emotions (vocabulary)
  • regulation through the body (breath, movement)
  • awareness of intensity (thermometer)
  • focus and settling (the jar)
  • positive memory and attachment
  • theory of mind (others have feelings)
  • releasing energy + freezing

A note for the parent: this week isn't about making your child “calm” or “in a good mood.” All feelings are welcome — anger, sadness, fear too. The goal is to give your child the words and tools for what they already feel. Don't push for results — the seed is planted now, the harvest comes years later. What you measure across these seven days isn't a change in behavior, but moments of connection.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #7 · getscreenfree.com Day 1 / 7
1

Three Faces in the Mirror

Happy, sad, angry — 3 minutes, 50 years.

The face shows what we feel first. The word comes after.

⚡ WOW Mission

Three Faces in the Mirror

Materials: a mirror (or just face to face), 5 minutes.

How to play

Stand in front of a mirror (or sit face to face). Say: “Let's make a happy face.” Make it big — wide smile, bright eyes. Your child copies. Then: “Now a sad face.” Drooping corners, slumped shoulders. Then: “Now an angry face.” Furrowed brow, clenched teeth. No explanation, no story — just face + word. You can add surprised, tired, scared. Keep it short, 5 minutes — repeat throughout the day. Imitating expressions activates mirror neurons — for a moment your child actually feels that emotion as a safe, controlled exposure. Three minutes today → words they'll use for the next 50 years.

🔧 Medium

Guess Which Face

You make a face without speaking. Your child guesses: “Happy? Sad? Angry?” Then switch — your child makes a face, you guess. Practice in reading other people's feelings on their face — the foundation of empathy.

🌿 Easy

Face Like Mama

Just two faces: happy and sad. You make it, your child copies. No guessing, no talking — just imitation. For younger children, copying one of your expressions is enough to feel what it means.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #7 · getscreenfree.com Day 2 / 7
2

Feelings Storybook

The picture has a face too — not just an action.

How is she feeling in this picture?

⚡ WOW Mission

What Is the Character Feeling?

Materials: any picture book from your house.

How to play

Instead of following the plot, on each page ask one question: “How is she feeling in this picture?” Your child answers (or points to their own face if they don't have the word). You name it and stretch it out: “Yes, she looks sad. See, she has tears. Why do you think?” If your child doesn't know — you offer: “Maybe because she lost her toy.” This is dialogic reading focused on the emotional category. Studies show that children whose parents often talk about characters' feelings develop significantly better theory of mind by age 4 — the ability to understand that other people have thoughts and feelings different from their own. The same book you've read 50 times reveals an entirely new layer.

🔧 Medium

What Now? What Next?

Follow one character across a few pages. Ask: “How did she feel at the start? What changed? How does she feel now?” Practice tracking an emotion through time.

🌿 Easy

Happy or Sad?

Two categories, nothing more. You show a page: “Happy or sad?” Your child points. For a younger child this is enough — the foundation you build on later.

3

Breathing with Teddy

Teddy rides up and down.

Belly lifts teddy up. Then sets him down. Slowly.

⚡ WOW Mission

Teddy on the Belly

Materials: a small teddy bear (or any soft stuffed animal), a place to lie on your back (bed, floor, blanket).

How to play

Your child lies on their back. Place a small teddy on their belly. “See teddy? He loves to ride up and down on your belly. Breathe in deep — up. Now slowly let it out — down.” Take 5–6 breaths together. You can sing slowly: “Uuup... dooown...” Then switch — teddy rides on your belly while your child watches. A slow exhale activates the parasympathetic nervous system — it physiologically calms. The most powerful part: the word “teddy” becomes a tool for future moments of meltdown. Months later, when your child is in a tantrum, that name calls up the learned settling response.

🔧 Medium

Counting Breaths

Your child holds teddy on their belly. You count: “One... two... three...” Then your child counts their own breaths — how many can they take quietly to the goal? Practice in attention and breath control at the same time.

🌿 Easy

Teddy Sleeps

No technique. Your child lies down, teddy on the belly, you watch together as he rises and falls. Don't try to do anything — just notice. Plenty for very young children to feel connected to their breath.

4

Feelings Thermometer

A little angry. Big angry. All angry.

A feeling can be small. A feeling can be big.

⚡ WOW Mission

Draw the Thermometer

Materials: paper; markers (blue, yellow, orange, red).

How to play

On a piece of paper draw a simple vertical thermometer — bottom to top, three or four levels. Bottom: calm (blue, a flat line). Middle: a little upset (yellow, a small wave). Higher: very upset (orange, zigzag). Top: explosion (red, a star). First show it on yourself: “Right now I'm here (at the bottom). I'm calm.” Then improvise situations: “Where do you think I am when somebody knocks down my block tower?” Your child points. “And you? Where are you right now?” This gives your child a visual language for intensity of feeling — which is harder than naming the emotion itself. A 2-year-old experiences everything as 100%; the thermometer introduces nuance. You also get a tool for future moments: “Where are you on the thermometer right now?”

🔧 Medium

When Is Mama on Red?

Talk through situations from your day: “This morning when the phone rang a bunch of times — I think I was on orange.” “And you, when daddy took the shovel?” Your child links a memory to a level. Practice in reflection.

🌿 Easy

Show the Color

No drawing. Three pieces of paper: blue, yellow, red. You ask: “How are you feeling right now?” Your child points to a color. Simpler for younger children when there's no complex scale.

SCREENFREE · Weekly System #7 · getscreenfree.com Day 5 / 7
5

Silly Dance

Then freeze! Don't move!

The body comes out — then stops. Then again.

⚡ WOW Mission

Dance and Freeze

Materials: any fast, happy music.

How to play

Turn on the music. “Let's do a silly dance!” Wave your arms, legs, head, tongue, all at once. No rules, no style. Your child copies you or makes their own. Then stop the music — “FREEZE!” Stand like statues 3–5 seconds. Then move again. Then freeze again. Ending: lie on the floor and “melt.” The combo of physical activation and intentional deactivation (“freeze”) is practice in interoception — the ability to feel your own body. Interoception is the foundation of self-regulation. Each “freeze” teaches your child to notice the difference between movement and stillness, which is the precursor to noticing the difference between “I'm angry” and “I'm calm.” It looks like pure laughter, and it's actually a technique used by child therapists.

🔧 Medium

Freeze-Game Rules

Add rules: “Freeze like a frog!” “Freeze with your hands up!” “Freeze on one foot!” Your child plans the move before stopping — practice in self-control.

🌿 Easy

Fast–Slow

No music, no freeze. Just “fast!” then “slow!” Run, then walk like a snail. Your child learns that movement can be fast or slow — the precursor to pacing themselves.

6

Calm-Down Jar

The glitter settles. And so do I.

It swirls. Then slowly settles.

⚡ WOW Mission

Calm-Down Jar

Materials: a clear jar or screw-top plastic bottle; warm water; 1 spoonful of liquid glue or glycerin; a few drops of food coloring (blue or purple); a handful of fine glitter or paper confetti.

How to play

Fill the jar 2/3 full with warm water. Add a spoonful of liquid glue (or glycerin), a few drops of color (blue/purple — calming), a handful of glitter. Close it and seal the lid shut (safety first). Shake — the glitter swirls and settles over 1–2 minutes. Your child watches, holds the jar, focuses on the settling. Visual focus on slow settling activates outward attention — which interrupts the inner emotional whirl. Plus, settling is a metaphor your child physically understands: “When I'm upset, my feelings swirl. If I wait, they settle.” An abstract idea a 3-year-old can literally see. The jar stays in the house for months as a tool — your child reaches for it themselves when they feel like it's too much.

🔧 Medium

Watch Until It Settles

You shake the jar. Your child waits and says “Stop!” when all the glitter has settled to the bottom. Patience + visual return of attention. You can add: “How do you feel now?” — your child often answers “calm.”

🌿 Easy

Hold the Jar

No shaking. Your child holds the jar in their lap and just looks at it. A quiet, calming moment — perfect before bed or after a hard situation.

7

Three Things from the Heart

It made me happy when...

Three small joys. The whole day.

⚡ WOW Mission

Three Things in My Lap

Materials: your lap, quiet, 5 minutes before bed.

How to play

Toward the end of the day, sit with your child in your lap or beside you: “Let's tell each other three things from the heart.” You start: “It made me happy when you hugged me this morning.” “I laughed when you said the dog has a mustache.” “I'm proud that you put your shoes on by yourself.” Then your child: “What about you? What's in your heart today?” They say as much as they know, as much as they want. If they don't know, you help: “Maybe when we ate ice cream?” No pressure, no “right answers.” An adaptation of the “highs and lows” family ritual — proven to be linked to secure attachment. Focus on the positive: at 2–4, positive memories are easier to process before sleep. It becomes a family ritual that survives long after this week.

🔧 Medium

What's in Your Heart?

No list from you. You ask your child first: “What was the best part of today?” Then you answer the same question. Practice in reciprocity — I hear you, you hear me.

🌿 Easy

One Thing

Just one thing. “What was the best part of today?” Your child says a word or two. You expand: “Yes, I remember how you laughed at the park.” The simplest version, perfect for very tired or younger children.

“Put teddy on your belly — breathe.”

“Shake the jar — let's watch together.”

“Show me a happy face.”

“Let's do a silly dance!”

“Freeze! Three seconds.”

“What's in your heart right now?”
